## What it does

Borrowers and loan officers on a mortgage-lending platform upload closing
packets, pay stubs, bank statements and dozens of other document types.
Getting structured, field-level data out of them reliably — not "usually" —
is the difference between straight-through processing and a human re-keying
everything by hand.

## The shipped piece

The incumbent OCR vendor did real work, but a large share of documents came
back classified and never extracted — the vendor knew what the document was
and had no schema to read it with. I originated the production LLM
extraction service that covers exactly that gap: it fires when the vendor
fails to classify or extract, and it has been in production since February
2026.

Mine from the first commit onward: the rearchitecture into three
independently retriable message-bus consumers with their own job records,
the migration off a hosted LLM gateway onto direct provider APIs, and the
multi-provider failover package that other services now share.

## The evaluation, kept separate

Whether the vendor could be replaced altogether is a different question, and
I answered it as a proof of concept rather than a deployment — eight rounds
over four weeks, sole author, never shipped. Every figure below is reported
with the sample it came from:

- Splitting a multi-document PDF into its constituent documents: **94.0%**
  segment F1 and **95.6%** page-assignment accuracy (n=250 ground-truth
  documents, scored deterministically, no LLM judge).
- Classification: **88.4%** exact document type, **95.0%** type family
  (n=242 matched pairs).
- Field reading: **74.7%** correctness over the **48%** of the answer key
  the scorer could actually compare (n=202 documents). The uncovered half is
  a live caveat, not a rounding detail, and the correctness figure should
  never be quoted without it.
- Cost: about **$0.30 per loan** on the reviewed corpus, against the
  incumbent vendor's flat **$25**.

Ground truth was drafted by review agents reading every page against the
production extraction, then reviewed and accuracy-confirmed by the
operations team, with a deterministic hygiene audit run over the answer key
itself.

## The finding I would lead with

Classification was a vocabulary problem, not a capability problem. Asked to
name a document type in free text, the model matched the target vocabulary
**14.3%** of the time. The same model, constrained to a closed 81-type
enum, reached **82.4%**. The fix was the output contract, not a bigger
model — and that result generalizes well past this pipeline.

I also disproved my own headline. Round one produced a dramatic accuracy gap
in my favour; a second corpus on the vendor's home turf showed it was corpus
bias, so I retracted the accuracy argument and rebuilt the case on coverage
and cost. A later round found a scoring bug that had inflated a number I had
already published internally, and I withdrew that too.

## Why it mattered

Every claim here is backed by a measured result on real documents, with its
sample size and its caveats attached, and the shipped work is stated
separately from the experiment. That distinction — an AI feature that is
tested, scored and cost-traced like any other production system, and honest
about which parts are live — is the throughline of most of what I have
shipped with LLMs.
