Replacing unreliable LLM checks with deterministic validation and bounded AI observations

Situation: One of our early LLM-enabled services analyzed structured data extracted from loan documents and generated a variable list of checks intended to alert loan processors to potential problems. After the system had been in production for an extended period, our product manager shadowed the processing team and discovered that nobody was using those checks. Follow-up conversations showed why: their number and wording varied, some were unclear, and others hallucinated information.

Task: I was asked to determine how to make the analysis useful and trustworthy.

Actions:

  • I considered two paths: keep the generic LLM checks and invest in representative golden-set evaluations, or replace the operational checks with deterministic code.
  • I recommended the deterministic approach, even though the team initially saw moving away from LLM-generated checks as a regression. The extracted fields already had known structures, and modern agentic coding tools had changed the implementation economics: deterministic checks and tests could now be created and maintained much faster.
  • I also compared the continuing model, evaluation, and runtime costs of the LLM approach, as well as the privacy controls required to prevent real-document PII from entering evolving golden sets.
  • In less than two weeks, I removed the open-ended LLM overview from every supported document type and expanded deterministic coverage across the service.
  • I preserved a narrower role for AI: capped, evaluated observations could provide up to three complementary suggestions beyond the authoritative deterministic checks.

Results:

  • The product manager reported successful results from follow-up interviews with the processing team.
  • The authoritative checks became stable, reproducible, and covered by deterministic tests instead of changing with model output.
  • The design removed unnecessary LLM runtime cost and reduced the need to maintain sensitive golden-set data for rules that could be expressed directly in code.
  • We retained LLMs where their flexibility added value, but no longer allowed open-ended generations to define the operational checks processors were expected to trust.

Preventing a monolithic AI pipeline from becoming a reliability problem

Situation: While preparing to migrate House Numbers' document-analysis checks from generic LLM output to deterministic rules, I reviewed the architecture that would carry the change. Party detection, document-specific checks, and checks involving multiple documents were all executed through one large orchestration. The pipeline had not caused a production outage. However, I recognized the risk before adding more complexity. The monolith was difficult to test and maintain, and a slow LLM call or transient failure late in the process could delay or repeat work performed earlier.

Task: I had seen similar long-running, LLM-intensive workflows become operationally difficult in other services, so I decided to improve the architecture before attempting the larger migration.

Actions:

  • I mapped the workflow and divided it into three independently testable units connected through the message bus: party detection, checks for an individual document, and cross-document checks.
  • Each stage persisted its progress before triggering the next one. This allowed a failed stage to be retried independently instead of replaying the full analysis.
  • I also designed the writes to tolerate repeated delivery and added focused unit and integration tests around each boundary.
  • Manual reanalysis was later moved into the same pipeline instead of maintaining a separate execution path.

Results:

  • The redesigned architecture and the subsequent deterministic-check migration both reached production.
  • Earlier stages could complete and preserve their results while downstream work continued.
  • The smaller boundaries also made it easier to locate failures, maintain the checks, measure each part of the workflow, and optimize individual stages without treating the entire analysis as one opaque process.

Redesigning a public document-upload flow around privacy and traceability

Situation: At House Numbers, borrowers used a public WordPress form to submit sensitive loan documents without creating an account. The downstream email workflow worked well: it received the documents, used an LLM to extract identifying information, and passed the submission into our existing triage process. The problems were in the client-facing WordPress portal. It was unreliable and offered very little traceability, so borrowers could not tell what had happened after submitting their files and our support team had difficulty reconstructing reported failures. Operationally, we estimated that roughly two out of every three submissions ended in a borrower complaint or support escalation.

Task: I proposed replacing the public portal for an extended period. Once the migration was approved, I researched the requirements and designed and implemented the replacement, including its security architecture. We needed to accept files from an unauthenticated public user without exposing borrower information, loan identifiers, tenant details, routing decisions, or other internal platform state.

Actions:

  • I designed the public boundary so that access was validated before a request reached internal services. The browser received an opaque session rather than internal loan or tenant identifiers, and client-provided identity information was removed before the request was forwarded.
  • I added CAPTCHA verification, scoped and global rate limiting, upload-size and file-count limits, replay protection, and defensive handling for malformed or expired sessions. Public responses were deliberately minimal so they could not reveal whether a loan had been found or which internal processing path had been selected.
  • The simplest implementation would have sent every submission through the existing email workflow. However, uploads already had significant volume, and that path incurred an LLM extraction cost for every submission. The new form collected the relevant matching information as structured fields, so using an LLM to extract the same information again would have added unnecessary cost and processing.
  • I instead designed a hybrid routing model. When the submitted information contained strong enough signals to associate the documents with a loan, deterministic rules performed the match directly without involving the LLM or Communication Service. When the signals were incomplete or ambiguous, the system preserved the proven email workflow as a fallback so operations could review the submission manually. This reduced unnecessary LLM processing while ensuring that an uncertain match became a human decision rather than a potentially unsafe automated assignment.
  • Every session and file submission produced a correlated audit history that followed the request from the public form through routing and document processing. Sensitive fields were encrypted, while structured logs retained identifiers and processing outcomes without unnecessarily copying raw personal information. I also created a diagnostic skill that uses this history to reconstruct an upload's journey across the participating services.

Results:

  • The new page began handling many uploads every day, and borrower complaints about failed uploads stopped.
  • Internal support reports still occurred, but we could investigate them quickly. In the cases we reviewed, the trace showed that the upload had succeeded and that the files had later been renamed, archived, or moved within the operational workflow. Instead of guessing whether the original submission had failed, we could show what had happened and direct support to the files.
  • I have applied the same principles elsewhere in the platform: tenant-scoping sensitive reads as defense in depth, removing raw PII payloads from logs, and preventing evaluation artifacts containing real document values from entering source control.

Tracing a production crash loop to an environment-specific schema failure

Situation: At House Numbers, a loan processor reported that none of the documents they had uploaded were being processed and that many appeared as "Unknown." The service had restarted 96 times in 24 hours. Its normal range was zero to four restarts per day. The immediate trigger was a webhook carrying a document identifier that our database did not contain. Several operations handling that identifier failed in parallel, and the first rejection masked sibling failures that remained unhandled. Those unhandled errors terminated the process, ECS replaced the task, and each restart could interrupt uploads already in progress. That created a feedback loop capable of producing more missing records and more crashes.

Task: I had the deepest familiarity with that document-processing path, so I took ownership of the incident and began tracing it from the production symptoms backward.

Actions:

  • I first contained the cascade. A missing document identifier became a recoverable error that was logged and skipped, and parallel work was allowed to settle without leaving unhandled failures. I also added instrumentation around the upload and persistence boundaries so I could see whether the external system had accepted a file and whether our database had recorded the returned identifier.
  • That evidence led me back to a recent major-version migration from the previous Zod release to Zod 4. The migration changed how some schemas treated absent values, and this response schema still relied on the earlier behavior. Local development and the production container also resolved different Zod 4.x versions because the production build did not install from a frozen lockfile. A response field accepted as absent locally was rejected in production. The external processor had successfully accepted the documents, but our application swallowed the validation error before saving their identifiers. When later webhooks arrived, the identifiers looked orphaned. Every document uploaded through the affected production build encountered this failure.
  • I reproduced the production error against a real failing response, matched its validation output locally, and changed the schema to preserve backward compatibility. I also changed the extraction path so future upload failures would reach the message bus retry and dead-letter mechanisms instead of disappearing inside a catch block. Regression tests covered each valid response shape, the parallel-failure case, and replay behavior. Persistence was made idempotent when a replay returned an identifier the system had already seen.
  • After deploying the correction, I identified the full affected window, prepared and executed the reconciliation work, and reprocessed the documents from that window. I then checked the remaining "Unknown" records in the database to distinguish unrecovered incident failures from legitimate cases where the external system had not sent an update or could not classify the document.

Results:

  • The cascade was contained and the failing path became observable and retryable instead of terminating the process.
  • The documents from the affected window were reprocessed, and the remaining "Unknown" records were checked to separate unrecovered incident failures from legitimate cases.
  • The incident caused no permanent data loss.

Preserving financial continuity during a legacy SAP migration

Situation: I was the backend engineer on a project rebuilding a procurement platform whose historical quote and operational data lived in a legacy SAP system. The migration needed to preserve more than individual field values: users expected financial calculations in the new platform to remain consistent with the system they already trusted.

Task: I owned the backend migration strategy and implementation.

Actions:

  • Before moving any data, I mapped the source records, relationships, calculations, and corresponding target models.
  • I then designed and implemented custom ETL scripts to extract, validate, transform, and load the data, with automated tests before cutover.
  • We added human verification by experts who understood the legacy system. They detected that, although the visible source values matched, some derived financial totals differed.
  • I investigated the extraction, mappings, and calculations and traced the discrepancy to different rounding behavior between the legacy system and JavaScript. I changed the new calculations to reproduce the established legacy rules. The goal was behavioral continuity for users, not proving that one rounding convention was theoretically better.
  • For the final migration, operations were frozen during a controlled extraction and load window so the source could not change underneath the process.
  • I also created an on-demand reconciliation process and took database snapshots before reconciliation runs, giving us a rollback point if an update produced corrupted or inconsistent data.

Results:

  • The migration was reviewed and approved by the legacy-system experts.
  • It completed without data loss or corruption, and after the MVP launched, users stopped relying on the legacy SAP system.
  • The reconciliation and snapshot strategy remained available for safe post-launch corrections without putting the migrated dataset at unnecessary risk.
